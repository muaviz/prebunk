## The Reporting Field Guide (second PDF)

This was not on the website. It is a dedicated research document GNCI prepared specifically to help builders understand the reporting ecosystem. It is the most useful planning resource they released and signals where their thinking lives.

### The Central Claim

Hate moves through four spaces and **loses context at every step**:

- Public feeds → Recommenders → Communities → Private forwarding
- With each step: who was targeted, what came before, how often it repeated, and whether it was coordinated all become harder to trace.

Their core instruction: **"Pick one step where content changes hands and keep the context alive across it."**

### The Five Moments Where a Team Can Step In

This is their implicit project brief, presented as a menu of where to intervene:

| Moment | What's possible |
| --- | --- |
| **Before spread** | Verify rumours, prompt creators, add friction to forwarding |
| **At the report** | Safe capture, help choosing the category, redaction |
| **During review** | Context retrieval, multilingual triage, routing of uncertain cases |
| **After the decision** | Appeal packs, status tracking, watching for copies |
| **During a crisis** | Cross-platform view, trusted escalation, fast corrections |

Their instruction: **"Choose one moment. Show the before and the after."**

### The Four Stages a Rule Must Survive

A hate response system only works if it survives all four:

1. **Rule** — What does the policy actually promise?
2. **Route** — Can the right post, with its context, reach a reviewer?
3. **Review** — Does the reviewer have the language and the context to judge it?
4. **Outcome** — Does the reporter learn what happened, and what to do next?

Their note: **"The gaps between these stages are where most hackathon projects live."**

Their most direct design instruction in the whole deck: **"The biggest gap sits between a report being filed and a real decision being made. Build there."**

### What Helps vs What Still Fails (Their Problem Brief)

The synthesis slide is effectively the problem statement they want you to solve. Commit this to memory:

**What works:**

- Specific reporting items (report attaches to the exact post, clip, or message)
- Human review with context (trained reviewers with the language and the thread in front of them)
- Time-bound crisis escalation (fast lane with an owner and a clock when harm moves quickly)
- Appeals and corrections (decisions can be revisited, corrections travel)
- Cross-platform collaboration (shared signals, e.g. GIFCT hash-sharing)
- Outcome data (reporters and partners see what happened and what changed)

**What still fails — these are the open problems:**

- Coded or visual hate slips through keyword filters
- Context is stripped at each handover
- Reporters hear nothing back
- Local languages and regions are blind spots
- Content is removed while the network continues operating

Their instruction: **"Pick one gap on the right and close it with one practice on the left."**

### The Five Forms of Hate

Important because "Synthetic" is new and the website doesn't mention it:

1. **Explicit** — Slurs, exclusion, dehumanization
2. **Coded** — Dog whistles, substitutions, emojis
3. **Visual** — Symbols, text inside images, templates
4. **Relational** — Reply swarms, repeated targeting of one person
5. **Synthetic** — AI-generated scenes presented as evidence ← new and growing

Their note: **"The coded ones slip past keyword filters. Treat words as signals. Combine the forms, and send the uncertain cases to trained people."**

### Platform-by-Platform Build Questions

GNCI posed a specific design challenge for each major platform. These are their actual questions — answering one of these directly maps to a winning project:

- **Meta:** How could a reporter keep the post, the reply chain, the target and the context together in one package?
- **YouTube:** How could a tool keep the clip, the title, the timestamp and the copies together, while the harmful video stays on the platform?
- **TikTok:** How could a workflow capture the video, captions, sound, comments and speed of spread in one safe package?
- **X:** What evidence shows the difference between a single post and a coordinated reply swarm or pile-on?
- **Reddit:** How could a report show what local moderators can act on, what needs sitewide review, and what came back?
- **Discord:** How could a team surface a server-wide pattern while users stay safe and review queues stay manageable?
- **Telegram:** How could a tool document public-channel migration and mirror channels while protecting people in private spaces?
- **WhatsApp:** How could a person decide what to report, keep the context and find support, with their private chat staying private?

## Case Studies Presented

### Southport 2024

This was their primary worked example and appears to be the canonical case they will use to evaluate submissions mentally. Know it cold.

The sequence: A false identity claim about the attacker appeared within hours of the attack. It spread across platforms via recommenders and forwards. It produced real-world disorder and direct attacks on Muslim communities and mosques. The correction arrived after the story had already hardened.

Their framing: **"Keep time, source and movement together. The pathway matters more than any single post."**

Four things Southport showed builders can improve:

- **Speed** — The right update arrives while choices are still open
- **Context** — Source, uncertainty and conversation travel together
- **Handover** — Public evidence becomes a short, safe escalation to a trusted local actor
- **Feedback** — The people who acted see what happened next

Their design instruction: **"Choose one decisive moment and make the next action clearer for a real person."**

### Tell MAMA

Their example of good practice to model from:

A fake far-right Telegram channel ("Direct Action UK") tried to spark disorder. Tell MAMA monitored anti-Muslim and extremist channels and passed verified updates to police and counter-terrorism partners.

Their five-step model:

1. Monitor public channels
2. Verify claims and impersonation
3. Record the time, the source and the copies
4. Escalate what is actionable
5. Log the correction and the remaining risk

Their summary: **"Success is verified intelligence reaching the right people before the next wave."** This sentence is the closest thing they give to a definition of what winning looks like.

## Q&A Intelligence

**Video games / long-term media:** Mohammed Babiker asked twice whether solutions could focus on long-term media like video games rather than just social media. It was not shut down. Games are an explicit output type in the briefing. However the depth of the Reporting Field Guide signals that real-time online hate is their primary concern.

**Offline problems:** Muizudeen Kusimo asked what if the solution solves an "offline" problem that manifests online afterward. This was answered positively — offline-to-online pathways are in scope.

**IP/ownership:** Yasin Usman asked whether submitting means handing over solution ownership. The answer is no — the participant agreement covers this. Worth reading the agreement carefully before Saturday.

**Proposal vs final product:** mercan Yadikar confirmed the submission does not have to be the final version of the product — it needs to demonstrate what you built and that it works, not be a complete production-ready system.

**Registration vs signing are separate steps:** Confirmed in the briefing. Both are required for prize eligibility. Each team member signs individually.

## The Build Principle (Their Template)

The last substantive slide of the briefing gave a fill-in-the-blank structure they expect every strong entry to answer. Fill this in for Prebunk before Saturday and keep it visible during the build:

> **For** [mosque/community organisation coordinators with no dedicated comms staff]
> **who** [need early warning about which anti-Muslim narratives will reach their community this week, before those narratives have hardened into belief]
> **we will build** [a weekly narrative forecast and inoculation brief service]
> **so that** [they can brief their community 48 hours before a narrative peaks — based on peer-reviewed inoculation research showing 5–10% reduction in susceptibility from a single exposure]

Their reminder: **"Who it's for. What they need. What you'll build. How you'll know it worked."**
